# Elsewhere's idea graph works if kept grounded

Elsewhere's feature set is buildable by a small open-source team today, but only if "knowledge graph" means a cheap, lazily built map of topics and quote-grounded positions, not a full, LLM-extracted graph of everything. Source discovery and topic organization are the strongest parts. Open seed lists, blogroll crawling and cheap search APIs cover discovery. Embeddings, clustering and LLM labeling cover topics, and the research behind both is solid. The signature feature, showing an author's position and finding similar, contrasting and tangential writing, is also feasible. It is the riskiest piece, though: standard embeddings cannot tell agreement from disagreement, LLM stance detection on long essays has never been benchmarked, and the worst failure is misrepresenting what an author believes. For the architecture question, the evidence favors embeddings plus hybrid search over heavy GraphRAG, and a lightweight Issue → Position → Claim schema (from IBIS and Kialo) over formal argumentation models. "Jev" most likely refers to TypeSafe's new hosted "decision model" API, but that identification rests on thin sourcing, so please confirm what you meant. Product precedent sets two design rules. The graph should appear as topic pages and local neighborhoods, not as a global hairball. AI costs should be paid through the user's own API key or local models, never by a hosted free tier.

## Four of six features rate "viable now", one rates "viable with guardrails"

The vision has three headline features, and each one breaks into parts with different evidence behind them. The verdicts below combine findings from all the research streams. The sections after the table give the reasoning.

| Feature | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fallback timeline | **Viable, solved** | Mature open-source readers (Miniflux, FreshRSS, NewsBlur) already do this. Reuse one of them rather than rebuilding it. |
| AI source discovery (seed + expand) | **Viable now** | MIT-licensed seed list of about 30–35k small-web feeds, crawlable OPML blogrolls, Marginalia and Exa APIs at a few dollars per thousand queries. The bottleneck is precision, not cost. |
| Source graph ("related writers") | **Viable now** | Blogroll, link, co-citation and embedding edges are all computable locally. Homophily is a known side effect. |
| Topic/concept knowledge graph | **Viable now** | Embed, cluster, LLM-label is well validated. Concept co-occurrence graphs index at vector-RAG cost. |
| Author positions: similar / contrasting / tangential | **Viable with guardrails** | Each subtask has known LLM accuracy, but blog-length stance is unbenchmarked, embeddings are stance-blind, and misattribution is the main risk. |
| Archive resurfacing ("evergreen" posts) | **Viable, heuristic** | Little research exists. An LLM timelessness judgment plus in-graph inbound links is the practical proxy. |
| Assistant Q&A over your reading (RAG) | **Viable now** | Hybrid BM25 + vector search with a reranker wins on lookups. GraphRAG adds little for this. |

On discovery, the Kagi Small Web repository is MIT-licensed and plain text, so Elsewhere can bundle it as a seed corpus. It lists **more than 30,000 sites** ([TechCrunch](https://www.techcrunch.com/2026/03/17/kagi-small-web-human-authored-indie-internet-mobile-ios-android-devices/); [Kagi blog](https://blog.kagi.com/small-web)). Marginalia's search API offers free non-commercial keys under CC-BY-NC-SA ([Marginalia API docs](https://about.marginalia-search.com/article/api)). The Blogroll Network Map shows that crawling feed → OPML blogroll → feed already reaches **14,251 feeds** ([blogroll-network](https://blogroll-network.alexsci.com/)). Agentic web search is cheap: Exa charges **$4–7 per 1,000 searches** and gives **$10/month in free credit** ([Exa pricing](https://exa.ai/docs/reference/pricing)). A weekly discovery run therefore fits inside the free tier. What Exa's numbers don't settle is precision. Nobody has evaluated how well these APIs surface independent writers rather than SEO content, so the curated small-web lists should serve as priors and filters. There is also a known structural trap. Adamic and Glance's study of the political blogosphere found that blogs link mostly within their own communities ([ACM](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/1134271.1134277)), so pure link-graph expansion will reinforce clusters. Elsewhere should deliberately sample "bridge" sources and exclude feeds the user already follows. Google Reader's recommendations were criticized for exactly this, suggesting items from feeds already followed ([isidorsfugue](https://isidorsfugue.com/2013/03/google-reader-once-knew-me-so-well.html)).

## Topics organize well in three layers: entities, emergent clusters, user tags

The research supports organizing knowledge in three layers, each solving a different problem.

The first layer is **named entities linked to Wikidata**. Linking gives free canonical IDs, aliases and a class hierarchy, so "GPT-4" and "OpenAI's GPT-4" collapse into one node. ReFinED had the best F1 in an independent fair evaluation, **73.3% versus 67.7% for REL** ([Bast et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14937)). spacy-entity-linker runs fully offline from a local SQLite knowledge base of about 1.3 GB, with about 70% accuracy ([GitHub](https://github.com/egerber/spaCy-entity-linker)). Linking breaks down on niche text, though: on maintenance logs, ReFinED and BLINK fell to **F1 ≈ 0.1** ([arXiv 2507.22935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22935)). Wikidata also won't contain the emergent ideas small-web writers care about, such as "local-first software".

The second layer, **emergent topics**, covers those gaps. Pham et al.'s TopicGPT matched human-assigned categories far better than classical methods, with harmonic purity of **0.73 versus 0.59 for LDA and 0.54 for BERTopic** on Wikipedia data. Its refinement step, which merges near-duplicate topics, cut misaligned topics on the Bills dataset from **70.1% to 31.9%** ([TopicGPT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.01449)). The catch is cost and model strength. Topic *generation* needed GPT-4-class models, and Mistral-7B produced 1,418 overly specific topics. Topic *assignment* worked acceptably with Mistral-7B, which lost only about 6 points of purity. This split shapes the whole design. Use local embeddings and clustering for the cheap, incremental grouping. Call an LLM once per cluster to name it and periodically merge duplicates, never once per article. Assign new articles with embeddings or a small classifier between re-clusterings. Microsoft's TnT-LLM follows this pattern at scale: an LLM generates and refines a label taxonomy, then distills it into lightweight classifiers ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/tnt-llm-text-mining-at-scale-with-large-language-models/)). RAPTOR's recursive cluster-and-summarize approach gives a hierarchy for free ([ICLR 2024](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/poster/19034)). One engineering warning applies: BERTopic's incremental `partial_fit` lets topic IDs drift between runs ([GitHub #946](https://github.com/MaartenGr/BERTopic/issues/946)). Users should see stable, named concept nodes, with raw cluster IDs kept internal.

The third layer, **user tags**, is cheap and well grounded. Golder and Huberman's Del.icio.us study found "a remarkable stability in the relative proportions of tags" on each URL ([J. Inf. Sci.](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/GolderAndHuberman-usage%20patterns%20collab%20tagging.pdf)). That suggests a personal folksonomy can sit alongside the automatic labels without destabilizing them.

What kind of graph should these layers form? Full triple extraction ("subject–relation–object" for every fact) is possible, but it is not the cheap default. KGGen pairs LLM extraction with an explicit entity-resolution step. It recovered **66% of facts versus 48% for GraphRAG and 30% for OpenIE** on its own MINE benchmark, at about **$0.84 per million characters** ([KGGen](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09956v2)). At that rate, my estimate is roughly $0.004 per typical article, or about $6/month for a heavy reader on a flash-tier API. That is fine with a user's own key but not free. The same pipeline also dropped from 73% with Claude Sonnet 3.5 to 44% with Gemini 2.0 Flash, and unconstrained extraction explodes relation labels: GraphRAG produced **966 relation types for 981 edges**. The better default is Microsoft's LazyGraphRAG approach, a concept co-occurrence graph built from noun phrases with no LLM at index time. It costs the same to index as vector RAG, **0.1% of full GraphRAG**, and defers LLM work to query time ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag/)). For Elsewhere, that means concept nodes link to the articles that discuss them, and richer claim-level detail is generated only when a user opens a concept.

## Opinions need a different pipeline, because embeddings can't see stance

The vision's most distinctive promise, "if you like the author's position on X, find similar, contrasting and tangential writing", is where most naive designs would fail. Standard sentence embeddings are **topic-dominated and stance-blind**: "I love pineapple on pizza" and "I hate pineapple on pizza" land close together, a published failure that also covers negated statements ([EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1171.pdf)). Cosine similarity can find articles on the *same issue*. It cannot tell you whether they *agree*. A separate stance step is required: an LLM classifier, an NLI cross-encoder, or a stance-tuned embedding model.

The research provides a usable definition of these relations. Wachsmuth, Syed and Stein define the best counterargument as one that "invokes the same aspects as the argument while having the opposite stance." Their pre-LLM system picked the right one about **60% of the time among roughly 8 candidates** ([ACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1023/)). Applied to Elsewhere, **similar** means same issue and same stance, **contrasting** means same issue and opposite stance, and **tangential** means a different but adjacent issue in the concept graph. Each piece maps onto a task with known LLM accuracy. Zero-shot stance detection reaches roughly the level of fine-tuned BERT systems: **63–72 F1 on the VAST benchmark**, with fine-tuning raising Llama 3.1 from 63.0 to 72.8 ([arXiv 2405.11637](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.11637); [arXiv 2410.07083](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.07083)). Classifying whether one argument supports or attacks another is the most reliable link: fine-tuned 7B models reach about **0.90 F1** on debate-platform data ([ADBL2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.13015); [Kialo-Prime](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/pdf/doi/10.3233/FAIA241026)). Clustering many arguments into canonical points is also proven. IBM's Key Point Analysis matched arguments to key points with **mAP above 0.9** in the ArgKP-2021 shared task ([overview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.10577)).

The weak link is extraction from real blog prose. Fine-tuned 8B models now beat earlier state of the art on argument mining. Llama-3-8B, for example, reached **89.5 macro-F1** on persuasive essays, but only when the argument spans were already marked in the input ([COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.442.pdf)). On messy online comments, binary argument detection fell to **0.76 F1** ([review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/llms-for-argument-mining-detection-extraction-and-relationship-classification-of-pre-defined-arguments-in-online-comments)). The 2025 survey of argument mining notes that systems miss claims conveyed through metaphor or narrative framing. It also reports that chain-of-thought prompting "roughly doubled the rate of subtle hallucinations" in one study, and that even human annotators agree only at α = .63 on stance ([arXiv 2506.16383](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.16383v6)). No benchmark exists for stance or argument extraction on long-form blog essays. Elsewhere would be building on unmeasured ground.

Which representation model fits? The evidence favors a lightweight **IBIS-style schema**: an *Issue* (a canonical question), *Positions* (an author's stance on it, including "mixed/qualified"), and *Claims* (supporting or attacking reasons, each with a verbatim quote). Kialo's pro/con trees use the same shape, and non-experts already understand it. Toulmin's model is too fine-grained to extract reliably from blogs, because warrants are usually implicit. Dung-style formal argumentation computes which positions "survive" attacks, which a reader doesn't need. Polis and Community Notes are not argument models at all. They cluster *voters* by agree/disagree patterns ([Polis](https://www.e-revistes.uji.es/index.php/recerca/article/view/5516); [Birdwatch](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15723)). They would only become relevant later, if Elsewhere collected reader reactions to positions.

## Embeddings and RAG carry the load, GraphRAG mostly doesn't, and "Jev" is optional

**Embeddings** are the foundation and run comfortably on local hardware. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B scores about **64 on MTEB Multilingual** with a 32K-token context ([Regolo](https://regolo.ai/embeddinggemma-2-vs-qwen3-embedding-benchmarks.md)). EmbeddingGemma, at about 308M parameters, scores **61.15** and supports Matryoshka truncation from 768 down to 128 dimensions. It uses about 542 MB of memory under Ollama, versus about 5.8 GB for Qwen3-0.6B unless its context is capped (same source). API embeddings cost about **$0.02 per million tokens** at the low end ([Activepieces](https://www.activepieces.com/blog/openai-embeddings-api-pricing-in-2026-dated-and-checked.md)), so embedding 10,000 articles costs pennies. Long articles retrieve better when chunked: whole-document embedding scored worst of three strategies on SciFact (63.89 nDCG@10 versus 66.10 for late chunking) ([jina-ai/late-chunking](https://github.com/jina-ai/late-chunking)). A single document-level vector is still useful for "related article" edges. At personal scale, no vector database is needed. sqlite-vec brute-forces **100k 384-dimension vectors in about 68 ms** on a laptop ([Bambini](https://marcobambini.substack.com/p/the-state-of-vector-search-in-sqlite)), and that benchmark's author builds a competing extension, so allow for some bias.

**RAG** serves the assistant, and plain hybrid retrieval beats graph methods for most reader questions. The independent GraphRAG-Bench (ICLR 2026) found GraphRAG "often underperforms vanilla RAG." On its Novel dataset, reranked RAG scored **60.9 on fact retrieval versus 49.3 for Microsoft GraphRAG**. Graph methods won only on multi-hop reasoning and corpus-wide summarization. Microsoft GraphRAG's global search used **331,375 prompt tokens per query versus 879** for vanilla RAG ([arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690v3)). HippoRAG 2 was the standout graph method, with near-RAG token cost (1,008/query) and the best reasoning score. Han et al. reach the same conclusion: neither approach dominates, and routing between them works best ([arXiv 2502.11371](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v2)). For Elsewhere, then, the graph's value is mostly **as navigation, not as a better answer engine**. Use SQLite FTS5 plus vector search, fused and optionally reranked, for questions. Keep graph traversal for exploration and "what themes run through my reading" summaries.

**Graph storage** should be plain relational tables. Kuzu, the leading embedded graph database, was **archived on 10 October 2025** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/14/kuzudb_abandoned/)). A personal graph of 10k–1M nodes fits easily in node and edge tables loaded into memory for PageRank or community detection.

**"Jev" (identification uncertain).** I found no argumentation or decision-theory framework in the academic literature called Jev. The only match is **Jev by TypeSafe**, a hosted "decision model" in early access since September 2026. It returns typed answers (yes/no probabilities, multiple-choice distributions, ordinal scores) with what the vendor calls calibrated probabilities ([AI/ML API docs](https://docs.aimlapi.com/api-references/decision-models/typesafe/jev)). The evidence is one aggregator's docs plus a single review. That review notes the vendor's evaluation "is not an independent audit," and warns of option-order sensitivity, non-determinism and prompt injection through the input text: "Typed is not correct" ([wavect.io](https://wavect.io/blog/jev-ai-decision-model-review/)). If this is what you meant, it fits one narrow slot well: the **pairwise relation judge** that labels two extracted positions as same / opposing / related / unrelated / unknown. It is closed and hosted, though, so it should be one pluggable backend behind an interface. A local LLM with constrained JSON output can fill the same role. **Please confirm what you meant by "Jev."** If you meant a decision or argumentation model in general, the relevant candidates are IBIS, Kialo-style bipolar trees, Toulmin, and Dung frameworks, as discussed above. Multi-criteria decision analysis was not researched.

**Other tools worth using.** Wachsmuth's definition of a counterargument and the Touché 2025 debate-retrieval pattern support having an LLM generate search queries aimed at a specific stance, such as "essays arguing *against* strict visual hierarchy" ([CEUR](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4038/paper_390.pdf)). That lets "contrasting" reach beyond the user's own feeds. For ranking, the recommender literature points to diversity re-ranking ([Kaminskas & Bridge](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/diversity-serendipity-novelty-and-coverage-a-survey-and-empirical/)), a contextual-bandit exploration slot (LinUCB lifted clicks **12.5%** on Yahoo news, [arXiv 1003.0146](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.0146)), and user-facing controls. MovieLens users given popularity and recency sliders rated their recommendations much more positively ([Harper et al. 2015](https://grouplens.org/blog/putting-users-in-control-of-their-recommendations)).

## A recommended build: SQLite, local embeddings, user-supplied LLM

For a small open-source project, the evidence points to a layered system in which every LLM feature is an optional enhancement on top of a working zero-LLM baseline.

**Ingestion.** Don't rebuild feed fetching. Use Miniflux or FreshRSS as a headless backend, or embed a library. Miniflux's license is reported inconsistently, so check the repo. FreshRSS and Folo are AGPL-3.0, which binds any fork ([FreshRSS](https://github.com/freshrss/freshrss); [Folo](https://github.com/RSSNext/folo)). Default to the text the feed provides. Fetch full text under an honest, distinct User-Agent that respects robots.txt, because **56.4% of news publishers block at least one AI crawler** ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/cloudflare-drops-ai-opt-outs-from-118-sites-robots-txt-hasdata-finds/)) and Cloudflare blocks AI bots by default ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/control-content-use-for-ai-training/)).

**Storage.** Use one SQLite file holding app data, FTS5 search, sqlite-vec vectors at 256 Matryoshka dimensions, and node and edge tables. Postgres with pgvector can be the server option. Record the embedding model and dimension on every vector so users can re-embed later.

**Zero-LLM baseline.** This includes local embeddings (EmbeddingGemma or a context-capped Qwen3-0.6B), noun-phrase concept co-occurrence, offline Wikidata linking, kNN "related article" edges, blogroll and link source edges, and hybrid search. All of it runs on a laptop CPU.

**LLM layer (bring your own key or local).** One OpenAI-compatible client config, made of a base URL, key and model, covers Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, OpenRouter and vendor APIs. Inoreader shipped bring-your-own-key in April 2026, which shows the model works for a mainstream reader ([Inoreader](https://www.inoreader.com/blog/2026/04/choose-your-ai-platform-for-intelligence-features.html)). Use it for cluster labeling and consolidation (per cluster, not per article), for discovery query expansion, and for position extraction. A 4B model on CPU generates about **9–25 tokens/second** ([deemwar](https://deemwar-products.github.io/llama-cpu-benchmarks/article-engines); [insiderllm](https://insiderllm.com/guides/cpu-only-llms-what-actually-works/)). That is enough to process a day's new articles overnight, but my estimate is that a backlog of 10k articles would take 40+ hours on CPU. Backlog processing therefore needs a GPU or an API key.

**Position pipeline.** For each post, extract at most 7 positions, each with an issue phrase, a stance (pro / con / mixed), a one-line paraphrase and a verbatim quote. Drop any position whose quote doesn't match the source text by string comparison. Embed the issue phrases and cluster them into canonical Issues, KPA-style. Generate candidate pairs by nearest neighbor, then classify each pair with an LLM or Jev-like judge, without the user's preferences in the prompt. Tangential links come from concept-graph adjacency.

**UI.** Make topic pages the home for ideas, with similar / contrasting / tangential rails and a local 1–2-hop graph. Don't make a global force-directed graph the main navigation. In an Obsidian user poll, **59% never use the global graph and about 8% use it frequently** ([poll](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/ObsidianMD/comments/1hg9qr3/how_often_do_you_use_the_global_graph_in_obsidian)). That poll is self-selected and anecdotal, but a 40-person study also found no speed advantage for node-link diagrams over text lists ([core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/210608686.pdf)). GRAFS, by contrast, embedded a small knowledge subgraph inside a familiar faceted-search UI, and in a 20-person study users understood relations between concepts better than with the faceted baseline ([arXiv 2302.09448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.09448)).

## The key risks are misattribution, unmeasured accuracy, and maintainer cost

**Misrepresenting authors** is the risk that could sink the product's credibility. LLM opinion summaries rate well with humans while still over-generalizing and misrepresenting viewpoints ([arXiv 2211.15914](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.15914)). Summarizers "implicitly smooth disagreement and over-represent majority opinions" ([arXiv 2601.04889](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04889v1)). The mitigations are product rules. Never show an attributed position without its quote and link. Label positions as "Elsewhere's reading," not "the author believes." Allow a "mixed/unclear" stance, and let users correct attributions. A false "this author disagrees with you" is the most damaging error, so precision on contrasting pairs is the metric to watch.

**No benchmark covers Elsewhere's actual task.** Every stance and argument number above comes from tweets, student essays or debate platforms. Graph evaluations measure question answering with LLM judges, not how people browse. And when GPT-4 judged GPT-4's output, its scores came out inflated ([arXiv 2506.16383](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.16383v6)). The project needs its own small gold set, for example a few hundred hand-labeled position pairs from real feeds. It should use a different model for judging than for extraction.

**Untrusted input.** Blog text flows straight into LLM prompts, so prompt injection is a real risk for any judge model, Jev included ([wavect.io](https://wavect.io/blog/jev-ai-decision-model-review/)).

**Echo chambers and "consensus by volume."** Link graphs are homophilous. Counting articles per stance lets prolific bloggers dominate, the same criticism made of the Consensus Meter, which counts a case series the same as a randomized trial ([Aaron Tay](https://aarontay.substack.com/p/a-2025-deep-dive-of-consensus-promises)). Show stance distribution *with* source diversity, not raw counts.

**Sustainability.** Pocket closed in 2025 and Omnivore in 2024, the latter with about two weeks' notice and data deleted ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2025/05/22/mozilla-announces-shutdown-of-pocket/); [MacRumors](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/omnivore-shutting-down.2441423)). Artifact shut down because "the market opportunity isn't big enough" ([Systrom](https://medium.com/artifact-news/shutting-down-artifact-1e70de46d419)). Tiny Tiny RSS's sole maintainer quit over burnout, and the project survived only through a fork ([Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/tt-rss-shuts-down-but-the-project-lives-on-under-a-new-fork)). A hosted free tier with server-side LLM calls has no funding source. Self-hostable software with full OPML and graph export, user-paid inference, and a deliberately small novel layer is the durable shape.

**Licensing traps.** Marginalia's free keys are non-commercial (CC-BY-NC-SA). SciPhi's local triple-extraction model Triplex is CC-BY-NC-SA ([featherless](https://featherless.ai/models/SciPhi/Triplex)). Forking an AGPL reader makes Elsewhere AGPL. None of these blocks a free project, but each would constrain a hosted or commercial version later.

## Conclusion

The research changes how "knowledge graph" should be read in the vision. Elsewhere does not need a graph database, a triple store or GraphRAG. Its graph is really two indexes joined by a UI: a cheap concept index (embeddings, clusters, Wikidata entities, co-occurrence) and a sparse, quote-grounded index of positions. The independent benchmarks agree that the expensive graph machinery buys little for a reader, while the cheap version gives most of the exploration value. The open niche is real. No commercial or open-source reader found in this research maps positions across articles. Inoreader's entity "mentions" are the closest anyone has come ([Inoreader](https://www.inoreader.com/blog/2026/09/introducing-mentions-redesigned-search-and-smarter-monitoring-feeds.html)).

The sensible order is to ship discovery, topic pages and archive resurfacing first, since they are low-risk and well evidenced. Then prototype the position layer against a hand-labeled set of real blog pairs before exposing it. Whether "contrasting" works on nuanced, hedged essays is the one question no study answers, and Elsewhere's own evaluation set would be the first evidence on it. Before designing the judge component around "Jev," confirm whether you meant TypeSafe's API or a decision or argumentation model more generally.
