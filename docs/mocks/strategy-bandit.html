Note — Why do wildcards still get a slot?

Wildcards have the worst record: . A greedy ranker would never show one again, and Thompson sampling nearly agrees: on its draws alone a wildcard wins of slots, because tries are enough to be fairly sure the rate is low.

Compare , the most uncertain source right now: only shown, so its curve is wide (), and it wins of draws. That is exploration by uncertainty: sources get tried in proportion to the chance they could be the best, not on a fixed schedule. Well-tested sources become predictable; uncertain ones get looked at until they aren’t.

For a source that is firmly low, like wildcards, the exploration floor is what keeps the door open: now and then a slot ignores the draws. Your taste changes, and a wildcard that starts landing will widen its own curve again by winning. Drag the floor in Fig. 2 and watch the odds on the right.

Humane limits: the rail is always five items and then it ends, a wildcard is labelled as one, and the only signals are things you finished or skipped. Time in app is not a signal.

Appendix — The machinery

for each of 5 slots:
  if random() < floor:                     # exploration floor
      arm ← uniform(eligible arms)
  else:                                    # Thompson sampling
      for arm in eligible arms: θ[arm] ~ Beta(wins + 1, shown − wins + 1)
      arm ← argmax θ
  item ← best unused candidate from arm    # each arm brings its own candidates
  eligible ← arms with < 2 slots so far

on "finished" or starred:  arm.shown += 1; arm.wins += 1
on "skipped":              arm.shown += 1

Priors come from your reading history (data.js strategies). “Wins a draw” in Fig. 1 and the wildcard odds are Monte Carlo estimates over 4,000 draws, recomputed whenever a belief changes. The simulation uses the same code path as the button; its hidden reader finishes each item with the source’s true rate.
