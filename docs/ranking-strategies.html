Ranking ideas, not time

How Elsewhere could decide what to show you next: the knowledge graph underneath, the ways to pull candidates out of it, what other products have tried, and how to make discovery feel rewarding without turning it into a slot machine.

1Similarity is the floor, not the goal

"More like this" from your own feeds is what we can build first. On its own it is the wrong objective.

Embedding nearest-neighbours, which our eval just tuned, answers one question well: what else talks about the same thing? Shown on its own it produces a familiar failure: the same author three times, two near-duplicates, and nothing you didn't already half-know. Ziegler and colleagues showed back in 2005 that people rated deliberately diversified lists higher, even though the lists scored worse on accuracy metrics. Users don't want the most similar items. They want the most useful next item.

So the ranking problem is really several problems, one per surface, each with its own goal:

Related rail · in the reader

"What should I read after this?"

Goal: continuation. Same idea from other minds; extends, complicates or contradicts what you just read.

The edition · home

"What deserves my attention today?"

Goal: a finite, balanced daily read. Fresh posts, depth, one surprise, one from the archive.

Topic pages · the graph

"What's known and argued about X?"

Goal: map an idea. Key pieces, positions, adjacent topics, open questions.

Discovery inbox · new sources

"Who else should I be reading?"

Goal: expand the source graph. New writers near your interests, plus some beyond them.

Across all four, the qualities to balance are relevance, novelty (new to you), diversity (different from each other), serendipity (unexpected and good), depth, freshness and fairness to small writers. No single score captures all seven, which is why the design that follows is a pipeline with several sources and a re-ranker, not one formula.

3Generate, score, re-rank, slot

Every serious recommender separates "find plausible things" from "choose and order them". It's also the cleanest fit for our functional core.

4Ten ways to find candidates

Arranged by how far each one reaches from what you just read. Near sources are safe and dull; far ones are where the surprises are, and where the misses are too.

Near · built next

Embedding neighbours

kNN over voyage-4 vectors, other feeds preferred. Our eval scored the top models about 1.3/3 on a small corpus; quality rises with corpus size.

  • Cheap, robust, works on day one
  • Topic-blind to stance; clumps by author; echo-prone

Middle

Concept bridges

Two hops through a shared concept or entity: A → "local-first" → C. Finds pieces that share an idea but not vocabulary.

  • Explainable ("both discuss local-first software")
  • Generic concepts ("software") create junk bridges; weight by rarity (IDF)

Middle

Links & co-citation

Bibliometrics for blogs. Co-citation (Small, 1973): two posts linked by the same writers are related. Bibliographic coupling (Kessler, 1963): two posts that link to the same things are related. Connected Papers is built on this.

  • Human judgement, not model judgement
  • Sparse until the corpus and archive grow

Middle

Personalised PageRank

Random walks that restart at your starred and finished articles, wandering across all edge types (Haveliwala 2002; Pinterest's Pixie, 2018, serves billions of recommendations this way). Wherever walkers pile up is relevant to you, not just to one article.

  • Combines every signal in one principled score
  • Drifts to hubs unless degree-normalised

Far · later

Stance contrast

Same issue, opposite position (Wachsmuth et al. 2018's definition of a counterargument). The signature feature, gated on our own accuracy test.

  • The thing no other reader does
  • Misattributing a position is the worst error we can make

Far

Frontier & wildcard

Deliberately one step outside your known territory (section 8), or a high-quality piece from a distant cluster. Tuned by a bandit so it learns how much surprise you like.

  • Where serendipity comes from
  • Misses feel like noise; keep it to one labelled slot

Anywhere

Archive resurfacing

Older posts from your sources that match current interests, plus spaced returns to things you starred. Needs archive backfill.

  • Unlocks the "years of good posts lost in the timeline" problem
  • Needs an evergreen judgement; dated news resurfaces badly

Beyond your feeds

Web search

An LLM turns an article or open question into search queries (Exa, Marginalia) and the results feed Discovery: "essays arguing against strict visual hierarchy".

  • The only way to reach writers you don't follow yet
  • SEO noise; filter with the small-web seed list

Not available to us: collaborative filtering ("people like you also read"), the engine behind Spotify, Netflix and YouTube, because Elsewhere is single-user and local. Not useful for discovery: popularity and recency formulas like Hacker News's (points − 1) / (age + 2)1.8. They belong in the timeline, not the graph.

5Choosing a set, not a list of winners

The top five most-relevant items are usually five versions of one item. Re-ranking picks the best set.

Maximal Marginal Relevance

Carbonell & Goldstein, 1998. Greedily add the item that is relevant and unlike what's already chosen. One parameter, λ, trades the two off. It's ten lines of pure code and the obvious first step.

next = argmax λ·rel(d) − (1−λ)·max sim(d, chosen)

Determinantal point processes

The principled version: score a whole set by how much "volume" its items span. YouTube published production results with DPPs (Wilhelm et al., 2018). Worth it only if MMR proves too blunt.

Hard constraints

Simple rules people can see: at most one item per author in a rail, at most two per feed in an edition, no two items above 0.92 similarity. These fix most of the "five of the same" problem on their own.

Calibration

Steck, 2018: if 70% of your reading is software and 30% history, the edition should be roughly 70/30, not 100% software because software scores highest. It stops the strongest interest crowding out the rest, which is a quiet form of filter bubble.

6What others tried, and what they learned

Most lessons are about format and incentives more than algorithms.

7Why discovery feels good

You asked about dopamine. The neuroscience is real and it's useful, but the mechanism that matters most here isn't the slot machine.

Dopamine neurons don't fire for reward as such. They fire for reward prediction error: something turning out better than expected (Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997). A recommendation you saw coming does little. One that surprises you and is good is the jackpot. That's why serendipity feels the way it does, and why a list of obvious neighbours feels flat even when every item is relevant.

Curiosity runs on the same circuitry. When people were curious about the answer to a trivia question, dopaminergic regions lit up in anticipation, and they remembered the answer, and even unrelated information shown alongside it, better a day later (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014). Curiosity is a learning state, not just a craving.

Design for the reward of understanding more, not the reward of one more scroll.

We do

  • Finite editions that end
  • Surprise as one labelled slot
  • Visible reasons and user dials
  • Success = things finished and starred
  • Quiet: no notifications, no badges

We don't

  • Infinite feeds or autoplay-style "next"
  • Streaks that punish a missed day
  • Optimising time in app or opens
  • Rewarding outrage or clickbait titles
  • Hiding why something was shown

8Approaches nobody quite does yet

Combinations that fall out of having a concept graph and your reading history in one place.

1 · The knowledge frontier

Keep a model of what you know: concepts weighted by how much you've finished and starred about them, decaying slowly over time. Recommend items whose concepts are mostly known but not entirely, about one hop out. This turns Berlyne's curve and learning progress into a ranking feature:

frontier(d) = overlap(d, known) × (1 − overlap(d, known)) peaks at about half familiar

It explains itself well: "builds on queueing theory, which you know, and introduces Little's law."

2 · The edition

A finite daily paper with named sections: Front page (fresh), Deeper, Counterpoint, Elsewhere (wildcard), From the archive. It ends. Discover Weekly's ritual plus a newspaper's shape, and the most direct expression of "follow ideas, not time".

3 · Trails

A generated path of 4–7 pieces by different writers through one idea, from accessible to deep, ordered by the concept graph. Progress is visible and the trail finishes. Think of it as a syllabus assembled from the small web.

4 · Open questions

An LLM pulls 1–3 questions each article raises or leaves unresolved; questions are matched across articles. A topic page shows "unresolved threads", and a related rail can lead with a question instead of a title. This uses the information-gap effect honestly, because the answer is real.

5 · Debate cards

Two quote-backed positions on one issue, side by side, from different writers. Behind the stance-accuracy gate. A card that reads "Fowler says X; Wayne says not-X, because…" is the clearest version of the vision's "contrasting knowledge".

6 · A map that fills in

A local concept map where regions you've read become solid and the frontier glows faintly. Exploration becomes visible progress, the good part of game mechanics without points. Local only, no global hairball.

7 · Return to…

Spaced resurfacing of ideas you starred, at roughly 1 week, 1 month and 3 months, bundled with what has been written about them since. Your archive becomes a conversation that keeps going.

8 · Strategy bandit

Treat each candidate source as an arm in a multi-armed bandit. Thompson sampling learns which sources pay off for you (frontier picks get finished, wildcards get skipped), while keeping some exploration. Li et al. (2010) saw a 12.5% lift from a contextual bandit on Yahoo news.

9 · Author constellations

Writers placed by what they write about and who links to whom, with "bridge" writers highlighted: people who connect two of your interests. Bridges are the best discovery candidates, because they counter link-graph homophily (Adamic & Glance, 2005).

10 · Ask the graph

An assistant that answers "what have my writers said about X?" with hybrid retrieval, citing passages, then suggests the frontier pieces. The answer itself is a ranking surface.

11What I'd build, in order

Start with the cheap, explainable pieces; earn the right to the clever ones with our own eval harness.

Recommended first build

Related rail = kNN → MMR → caps → reasons

voyage-4 neighbours from other feeds, MMR with λ ≈ 0.7, one item per author, a "why" label on each, and finish/star logging from day one, because later learning needs history. It's all pure functions plus one vector index.

The bet

Frontier + edition

If one idea makes Elsewhere feel different, it's a finite daily edition ranked toward your knowledge frontier: reliable, a little surprising, and done in fifteen minutes. That's the version of "dopamine" worth building.
